Key terms & FAQ

Short answers to the questions people ask most, then every key term from A to Z. Click any line to open it. The small ? on the Map page opens the right answer here.

Common questions

What is a need tier?

A rating from 1 to 5 of how badly a block needs a public restroom; 5 is the greatest need. Blocks with an open restroom within a 5-minute walk are served and get no tier.

Every other block is rated on five reasons, and it takes its strongest one. Nothing is added up or weighted. The About page shows where each reason's data comes from.

What do "served", "partly served" and "gap" mean?

Served: an open restroom within a 5-minute walk. Partly served: 5 to 10 minutes; the tier is lowered by one. Gap: more than 10 minutes, so the block keeps its full tier.

Why does one reason set the tier on most blocks?

Each block takes its single strongest reason, the way DOT's Pedestrian Demand Map lets one busy street set a corridor's class. In busy areas, like most of Manhattan, foot traffic is usually the strongest reason.

Citywide, the default cut-offs hold every reason to the same bar: each is calibrated to be about as rare at each tier as DOT's street classes. Of the 609 tier-5 blocks, vulnerable residents set 292, poverty 165, 311 reports 114 and busy sidewalks 38. Tick Ignore foot traffic above the map to rank without foot traffic.

How are the score tiles rated Good, Fair or Poor?

Two tiles are rated against the City's own figure: 79.9% of New Yorkers live within a 10-minute walk of a public restroom (LL58 report, p. 8). Good means a district does at least that well (80% or more), Fair 60–79%, Poor under 60%. One tile counts residents; the other counts people walking by, so busy places with few residents, like Midtown or Hunts Point, are judged fairly.

The 20-minute tile checks that almost nobody is left far behind: Good is 99% or more, Fair 95–98%, Poor under 95%. The 5-minute tile and the count of tier 4–5 blocks are shown but not rated: being 5 minutes away is a convenience, not a standard, and rating it would mark the same low-density districts down twice.

What does FLUSH! do?

It places restrooms for you. The map puts one on the neediest block where one is allowed, counts it as open, scores every block again, and repeats. Because each new restroom serves the blocks around it, the picks spread out instead of piling up on one street.

Each district's Top 10 next sites, in the panel beside the map, are made the same way on your current settings.

Why are some restrooms not counted on the map?

A restroom counts when it is listed as open and its type is switched on. Hover over any dot on the map to see whether it counts, and why not if it doesn't.

  • Closed or not open yet: listed as not operational, closed for construction, closed, or planned.
  • Subway station restrooms: from a separate MTA list, with shorter hours (7 am–7 pm), so they are off by default. Switch them on under Settings › Which restrooms count.
  • Ruled out by a scenario you chose: seasonal restrooms in Winter, restrooms that close before 8 pm in Evening, and anything not fully accessible when Accessible restrooms only is ticked.
  • Switched off by you: click a restroom on the map to stop counting it. A banner says so, and Reset to defaults puts it back.
A restroom is missing or wrong. How do I tell you?

Use Report a restroom (also on the Restroom directory). Drop a pin or type an address, answer a few quick questions, and send it by email; you can also save it as a spreadsheet row or print a paper form for a community meeting.

The page stores nothing you type. A person checks every report against the City's list before anything on the map changes.

Can someone change the data in this map?

Not in a way that looks official. The public map cannot load restroom lists, boundary files or saved copies from anyone else, and only the project team rebuilds its data from the publishers' own copies. You can still try "what if" changes, like switching a restroom off, but the map then shows a banner saying the view has been changed.

What is a drop-in centre?

A site run by the NYC Department of Homeless Services where people without a home can rest, eat, shower and get connected to services. The city has nine of them.

People without a home address are not in the census figures, so the map gives any block within 800 metres (about half a mile, or 18 minutes on its walk rule) of a drop-in centre at least tier 4, as a reason of its own, whoever lives on the block. They show on the map as small diamonds.

What does "Ignore foot traffic" (Residents only) do?

It switches off foot traffic, so blocks are ranked only by the people who live there: vulnerable residents, poverty and 311 reports. Use it to plan for neighbours rather than for people passing through.

What do "Winter" and "Evening" mean?

The map normally assumes a typical busy day, when seasonal restrooms are open. Winter stops counting restrooms the City lists as seasonal.

Evening counts only restrooms whose listed hours run to 8 pm or later on most days of the week. Many park restrooms close at 4 pm, so evening gaps are much larger.

What does "Closed & planned" show?

Restrooms the City lists as closed, and the people who lose a nearby restroom while they stay closed. It also shows planned sites: places already approved or listed for a future restroom.

Planned sites come from the City's Local Law 58 report (Appendix A) and DOT's planned automatic toilets. A FLUSH! pick within 400 m of a planned site is flagged "already planned nearby".

What is a Throne unit?

A self-contained modular restroom that needs no water or sewer hookup, made by the company Throne Labs. In 2026 the City placed 17 of them in a one-year pilot, open 7 am–10 pm.

The Throne pilot page checks whether the map sees need where the City put them.

Which lens should I start with?

Siting. It answers the main question: where is a restroom needed, and where can one stand? The other lenses help you build the case around a site, and show what the City weighs when it sites restrooms in batches: zoning, flood risk, operations, who represents an area. A lens never changes a block's tier.

Can I use this to choose a site?

Use it to make a shortlist, not a decision. Tiers are estimates from public data, and the map can't see café or store restrooms, real walking routes or the exact spot on a block.

Every site still needs a visit on the ground and agency approval. How sure we are of each number is on the Numbers page.

What is the Parks inventory, and why are some Parks restrooms shown but not counted?

NYC Parks released its own list of restroom buildings under a Freedom of Information Law request: 662 buildings with 1,321 rooms, which are closed and why, open repair orders (all as of 1 Oct 2026), and every restroom building project since 2005.

The map checks it against the City's public restroom list (2 Nov 2026). 639 Parks buildings match a City listing. The City's list is newer, so it still decides what counts. Where the two disagree, the restroom's details say so.

23 Parks buildings are not on the City's list. They are shown with a dashed green ring but not counted, because they may be staff-only, seasonal or simply missing. 9 are listed as abandoned and get a grey ✕. If you know one of them, use Report a restroom.

What does "Projected work" show?

The 81 Parks restroom building projects that are not finished: 26 new buildings and 55 rebuilds or other work, from proposed through design, procurement and construction. Darker markers are further along.

Tick the phases you want counted, and the 2030 scenario treats those projects as open: a new building adds a restroom, and a rebuild reopens a restroom the City lists as closed. All phases are ticked to start. Proposed projects are not funded or scheduled, so untick them for a cautious view. The normal map never changes.

Where does the data come from, and how current is it?

Mostly NYC Open Data: the City's Public Restrooms list (2 November 2026), DOT's pedestrian demand map, the census, 311 and City Planning's tax-lot data. Every source and its date is on the Sources page.

Key terms, A to Z

311 reports

Public-urination complaints to 311 within 200 m of a block, per year. They show who reports as much as what happens, so they are one reason among four.

APT

Automatic public toilet: the self-cleaning street toilets run under DOT's street-furniture franchise.

BID

Business Improvement District: a local group of property owners that funds extra street services and could partner on a restroom.

Census block

The smallest area the census counts, often one city block. The map scores each one.

Clear path

The open sidewalk left beside a restroom. DOT asks for 8 ft in front of an automatic toilet and 5 ft on its other sides, so a sidewalk unit needs about 15 ft of sidewalk. The tool checks the widest sidewalk on the block, not the exact spot.

Community district

One of the city's 59 districts, each with a community board. Step through them with ◀ ▶ above the map, or the [ and ] keys.

Deciding factor

The one reason that set a block's tier: busy sidewalks (foot traffic), vulnerable residents, poverty or 311 reports. Ties between blocks go to more people on foot, then more residents.

Detour factor

How much longer a real walk is than a straight line, because you follow streets and go around blocks. The map uses 1.45, checked against 587 routed walks. It is distance, not waiting time.

Calibrated cut-offs

The default tier thresholds. They hold every reason to the same bar: each resident reason is about as rare at each tier as DOT's street types are across the city's blocks. The earlier round numbers are a preset under Settings › Tier thresholds.

FLUSH!

The button that places restrooms: one on the neediest allowed block, counted as open, then the next, so picks spread out.

Foot traffic

How busy the sidewalk is: DOT's five street types (Global, Regional, Neighborhood, Community, Baseline), or a busy subway station within 150 m. Estimated from DOT's pedestrian counts, not counted on every block.

Lens

A view of the map for one question, such as zoning, flood risk or who represents an area. It adds layers and a results panel and never changes a tier. Siting is the one to start with.

Need tier

How badly a block needs a public restroom, from 1 (least) to 5 (most). The block takes its strongest reason. Served blocks get no tier.

Parks inventory (FOIL)

NYC Parks' own list of its restroom buildings, rooms, closures, repair orders and building projects, released under a Freedom of Information Law request (Oct 2026). Checked against the City's list, which still decides what counts.

Planned site

A place already approved or listed for a future public restroom.

POPS

Privately owned public space: a plaza or atrium a building owner keeps open to the public in return for extra floor area.

Poverty

The share of residents below the federal poverty line (Census survey, by tract).

Projected work

A Parks restroom building that is proposed, being designed, out to bid or under construction. The lens of the same name shows them; ticked phases count as open in the 2030 scenario.

Score tiles

The five boxes above the map: residents and people on foot within 10 minutes, everyone within 20 minutes, within 5 minutes, and blocks in tier 4–5. The first three are rated Good, Fair or Poor (see the question above).

Served · partly served · gap

Served: an open restroom within a 5-minute walk; not ranked. Partly served: 5 to 10 minutes; the tier is lowered by one. Gap: beyond 10 minutes.

Service ring

A loop one cleaning crew drives, through several units and, where there is one, a Parks restroom as its base.

Site check (desk review)

What public data can tell about a site before anyone visits: land owner, sidewalk width, flood plain, nearby subway, who must approve. Sun, curb uses, below-grade structures and lighting need someone on site.

Top 10 next sites

The ten places FLUSH! would choose first in the open district, on your current settings, listed beside the map. Click one to see it.

Visits a day

An estimate of use for cleaning schedules and cost: 1% of people on foot, up to 215. Never used for ranking.

Vulnerable residents

The share of residents aged 65 or over, under 5, or under 65 with a disability (Census survey, by tract). A block within 800 m of a homeless drop-in centre counts as at least tier 4 as a separate reason, whoever lives there.

Walk time

The straight-line distance × 1.45 (the detour factor) at 64 m a minute: 3.5 ft a second, the design speed traffic signals are timed for (MUTCD). It is slower than most adults walk; the mobility-device preset uses 50 m a minute.
