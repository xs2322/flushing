1Where are restrooms needed?

All 59 community districts. * has a Throne restroom.

A need tier rates how badly a block needs a public restroom, from 1 to 5. Blocks within a 5-minute walk of an open restroom are served. Every other block takes its strongest reason: busy sidewalks, vulnerable residents, poverty or 311 reports. More on tiers

Most people can leave this on the default. Foot traffic follows DOT's street classes (Global 5, Regional 4, Neighborhood 3, Community 2, Baseline 1). Subway follows DOT's own rule: a station within 150 m with more than 50,000 riders a week (about 7,100 a day) makes it Regional (4); any other station, Neighborhood (3). Subway entries are in thousands a day; blank means none. Our earlier daily cut-offs are kept as a preset. Resident and poverty cut-offs are round numbers, the same in every district (see Numbers). "Equity first" lowers them; "Foot traffic first" caps them at tier 3. The 30-site list and the Throne check always use the default.

Walk and contract assumptions

The walk standard and speed decide who is served. The other three only estimate visits and cost for contracts; they do not change tiers. The share is an assumption until the city publishes Throne's New York use counts; the best public data are from Throne's Long Beach, California pilot, where the busiest unit averaged 119 uses a day. Cost is the Throne pilot: $4M for 17 units for a year, units and service together. Speed is 3.5 ft a second, the walking speed US signal timing uses (MUTCD 4E.06); a brisk adult is about 80. The detour factor turns a straight line into a street walk: routed walks on the city's streets average 1.45 times the straight line. Sources for every number: Numbers.

Choose the land a restroom may stand on. Land never changes a block's tier; it only decides where one is allowed. Each pick then gets a desk check of what the data can show.

3Where should they go?

The map places a restroom on the neediest allowed block, counts it as open, and repeats, so picks spread out. Each pick shows its site check and a suggested type of restroom.

To pick for a whole borough or the city, use the three-step picker on Shortlists.

Key terms
Need tier
How badly a block needs a public restroom: 1 (least) to 5 (most). Four questions each give a rating from 1 to 5; the block takes the highest. Nothing is weighted or added. Served blocks get no tier.
Served · partly served · gap
Served: an open restroom within a 5-minute walk; not ranked. Partly served: 5 to 10 minutes; the tier is lowered by one. Gap: beyond 10 minutes.
Deciding factor
The one factor that set the block's tier. Ties between blocks go to more people on foot, then more residents.
Foot traffic
DOT's five street classes (Global, Regional, Neighborhood, Community, Baseline), or a subway station within 150 m with many daily entries.
Vulnerable residents
Share of residents aged 65+, under 5, or under 65 with a disability (Census survey). A block within 800 m of a homeless drop-in centre counts as at least tier 4, as its own reason, whoever lives there. Resident shares only count on blocks with 10 or more residents.
Poverty · complaints
Poverty rate (Census survey) and yearly 311 public-urination complaints within 200 m.
Walk
Straight-line distance × 1.45 for real streets, at 64 m a minute (3.5 ft a second, the walking speed traffic signals are timed for).
Clear path
The open sidewalk left beside a unit. DOT's Street Design Manual asks for 8 ft in front of an automatic toilet (6 ft 7 in wide) and 5 ft on its other sides, so a sidewalk unit needs about 15 ft, or about 20 ft if the 5 ft applies at the curb. The tool checks the widest sidewalk on the block, not the exact spot.
Lens
A view of the map for one question, such as coverage, zoning or flood risk. It never changes a tier; it adds layers and a results panel.
Service ring
A loop one cleaning crew drives, through several units and, where there is one, a Parks restroom as its base.
Desk review · site visit
The tool checks what public data can show. Sun, curb uses, below-grade structures and lighting need someone on site.
Visits a day
1% of people on foot, up to 215, for people with no restroom nearby. Used for cleaning schedules and cost, never for ranking.

Data and sources
  • Restrooms: NYC Public Restrooms (open data) and the 17 announced Throne units
  • Foot traffic: DOT Pedestrian Mobility Plan street classes; MTA subway ridership
  • Residents: 2020 Census blocks; Census survey (ACS) for age, disability and poverty
  • Complaints: 311 service requests (public urination)
  • Land: MapPLUTO tax lots, parks and plazas; sidewalk widths from city planimetrics
