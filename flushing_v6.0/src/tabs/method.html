Method

A need tier rates how much a block needs a public restroom, from 1 to 5. Taking the strongest reason follows how DOT's Pedestrian Demand Map rates streets. The walk-distance step is FLUSHING's own.

  1. Distance first.An open restroom within a 5-minute walk: served, not ranked. 5 to 10 minutes: partly served, tier lowered by one. Beyond 10: a gap.
  2. Each reason sets a tier.Foot traffic, vulnerable residents, poverty, 311 complaints and a nearby homeless drop-in centre, each rated 1 to 5 with the cut-offs below.
  3. The highest rating wins.It is shown as the block's deciding factor. Nothing is weighted or added.

Ties go to the block with more people on foot, then more residents. A block can reach tier 5 on foot traffic alone, as in Midtown; tick Ignore foot traffic above the map to rank by residents alone.

Land and cost never change a tier. Land decides where a unit can stand; expected visits and cost are used for contracts.

Cut-offs are the same in every district, so a tier means the same thing everywhere. The resident and complaint cut-offs are calibrated to DOT's Pedestrian Demand Map: each tier is about as rare as the matching DOT street class. Resident factors count only on blocks with 10 or more residents, since survey shares come from the surrounding Census tract. A block within 800 m of a homeless drop-in centre counts as at least tier 4, as its own reason and whoever lives there. You can change them under Settings › Tier thresholds. The Throne pilot page always uses these defaults.

How sites are picked

The FLUSH! button and each district's top 10 follow the same steps every time.

  1. Score every block with the settings on the Map page.
  2. Take the block with the highest tier, and among equals the most people on foot.
  3. Find land. If a unit is allowed on that block (Settings › Where can they go?), it goes there. If not, it goes to the nearest block within 200 m that does. If there is none, the block is listed and the tool moves on.
  4. Count the new unit as built and sort every block again. Blocks near it become served, so the next pick lands somewhere else.
  5. Repeat until the number of sites asked for is reached, or no block is left with a gap.
  6. Check each pick at the desk: land owner, sidewalk clear path, flood plain, who must approve and what kind of restroom fits; then list what needs a site visit (sun, curb uses, below-grade structures, lighting).

Choices made for fairness

  • Access comes firstA block with a restroom nearby is served, however busy it is.
  • One strong factor is enoughA block with heavy foot traffic, or many older residents, or high poverty, rises on that alone, as DOT's map lets one strong generator set a street's class.
  • The walk is timed for a slower walkerAbout 64 m a minute (3.5 ft a second), the pace used for older pedestrians, so 10 minutes is about 640 m.
  • Land and cost do not lower needNeighbourhoods with little parkland are often the ones with the least access.
  • Small areas are steadiedSurvey figures from an area with few residents are blended with the city average.
  • Complaints are one reason among fiveThey show who reports as much as what happens. Arrest data is not used.
